The information below is an elementary overview of the basic properties of the gradient of a function and of the divergence and curl of a vector field. These topics are typically covered in a third semester calculus course.

The gradient

The gradient of a differentiable function of three variables $latex f(x,y,z)$ is defined by

$latex \hbox{grad}f=\vec \nabla f = \displaystyle \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \vec \imath + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \vec \jmath + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} \vec k$

and has the following properties.
  • $latex \vec \nabla f$ points in the direction where $latex f$ is increasing the fastest.
  • The rate of change of $latex f$ in that direction is equal to $latex ||\vec \nabla f||$.
  • $latex \vec \nabla f$ is perpendicular to the level surfaces of $latex f$ (i.e. to surfaces of equation $latex f=C$ = constant).
  • The directional derivative of $latex f$ in the direction of the unit vector $latex \vec u$ is equal to $latex \vec \nabla f \cdot \vec u$.
  • Critical points of $latex f$ are such that $latex \vec \nabla f = \vec 0$ at these points. Generic critical points are minima, maxima and saddle points of the function $latex f$.
  • The extrema of $latex f$ subject to the constraint $latex g(x,y,z)=C$ are points on the curve of equation $latex g(x,y,z)=C$ where the gradient of $latex f$ is parallel to the gradient of $latex g.$ The constant of proportionality is called a Lagrange multiplier.

Line integrals and gradient fields

Line integrals

The line integral of a continuous vector field $latex \vec F$ along the oriented path $latex \mathcal C$ is written

$latex \displaystyle \int_{\mathcal C} \vec F(\vec r) \cdot d\vec r,$

where $latex \vec r$ is the position vector and $latex d\vec r$ is tangent to the path $latex \mathcal C$. If we know a parametrization of $latex \mathcal C$, i.e. if $latex \mathcal C$ is drawn by $latex \vec r(t)$ when $latex t$ varies between $latex t_0$ and $latex t_1$, the above line integral reads

$latex \displaystyle \int_{\mathcal C} \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \vec F(\vec r(t)) \cdot \frac{d \vec r}{d t}\ dt,$

and can thus be computed.

Example 1

Consider the vector field $latex \vec F(x,y) = e^x \vec \imath + e^y \vec \jmath$. Let $latex \mathcal C$ be the part of the ellipse $latex x^2+4 y^2 = 4$, joining $latex (0,1)$ to $latex (2,0)$ in the clockwise direction. A parametrization of $latex \mathcal C$ is $latex x = 2 \sin(t)$, $latex y=\cos(t)$, with $latex t$ varying between 0 and $latex \pi/2$. Therefore,

$latex \begin{align*} \int_{\mathcal C} \vec F \cdot d\vec r & = \int_0^{\pi/2} \left[e^{2 \sin(t)} \vec \imath + e^{\cos(t)} \vec \jmath \right] \cdot \left[2 \cos(t) \vec \imath - \sin(t) \vec \jmath \right] dt \\ & = \int_0^{\pi/2} \left[2 \cos(t) e^{2 \sin(t)} - \sin(t) e^{\cos(t)} \right]\ dt \\ & = \left[ e^{2 \sin(t)} + e^{\cos(t)} \right]_0^{\pi/2} = e (e -1).\end{align*}$

Example 2

The work done by a force $latex \vec F$ along the path $latex \mathcal C$ is given by $latex W = \int_{\mathcal C} \vec F \cdot d\vec r$. If $latex \vec r$ moves according to Newton's law, $latex \vec F = m \ d^2 \vec r / d t^2$, so that

$latex \displaystyle W = \int_{t_0}^{t_1} m\ \frac{d^2 \vec r}{d t^2} \cdot \frac{d \vec r}{d t}\ dt = \left[\frac{1}{2} m \left(\frac{d \vec r}{d t}\right)^2 \right]_{t_0}^{t_1} = \left[ \frac{1}{2} m v^2 \right]_{t_0}^{t_1},$

i.e. the work done by $latex \vec F$ as a point mass moves along the path $latex \mathcal C$ is equal to the difference in kinetic energy of the point mass between the end points of $latex \mathcal C$.

Gradient fields

A vector field $latex \vec F$ is a gradient field if there is a function $latex f(x,y,z)$ such that $latex \vec F = \vec \nabla f$. The vector field $latex \vec F$ is then conservative, path-independent, and circulation free.
  • A path-independent vector field $latex \vec F$ is such that the line integral of $latex \vec F$ along any path in the domain of $latex \vec F$ only depends on the end points of the path.
  • A circulation free vector field is such that its circulation (i.e. its line integral along any closed curve) is zero everywhere in the domain of $latex \vec F$.
The fundamental theorem of calculus for line integrals tells us that a gradient field is path-independent, i.e.

$latex \displaystyle \int_{\mathcal C} \vec \nabla f \cdot d \vec r = f(Q) - f(P),$

where $latex \mathcal C$ is a (piecewise) smooth path joining $latex P$ to $latex Q$ and $latex \vec \nabla f$ is continuous on $latex \mathcal C$. Conversely, any path-independent vector field is a gradient field.

Example

Consider the force $latex \vec F = m \frac{d^2 \vec r}{d t^2}$ discussed above. If $latex \vec F$ has a potential function, i.e. if there exists a function $latex V(r)$ such that $latex \vec F = - \vec \nabla V$, we have

$latex \begin{align*} W & = \int_{\mathcal C} \vec F \cdot d\vec r = - \int_{\mathcal C} \vec \nabla V \cdot d\vec r = V(\vec r(t_0)) - V(\vec r(t_1)) \\ & = \left[ \frac{1}{2} m v^2 \right]_{t_0}^{t_1}. \end{align*}$

Thus, $latex \displaystyle \frac{1}{2} m v^2(t_0) + V \left(\vec r(t_0) \right) = \frac{1}{2} m v^2(t_1) + V \left(\vec r(t_1) \right),$ i.e. the total energy, which is the sum of the kinetic and potential energies, is conserved.
  • If $latex \vec F = F_1 \vec \imath+ F_2 \vec \jmath + F_3 \vec k$ is a gradient field with continuous partial derivatives, then

$latex \displaystyle \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial x}, \qquad \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial F_3}{\partial y}, \qquad \frac{\partial F_3}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial z},$

i.e. $latex \text{curl} \vec F = \vec 0$.

The curl

The curl of a vector field $latex \vec F = F_1 \vec \imath+ F_2 \vec \jmath + F_3 \vec k$ with continuous partial derivatives is a vector given by

$latex \begin{align*} \text{curl} \vec F & = \vec \nabla \times \vec F = \left \vert \begin{array}{ccc} \vec \imath & \vec \jmath & \vec k \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ F_1 & F_2 & F_3 \end{array} \right \vert \\ & = \left( \frac{\partial F_3}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial z} \right) \vec \imath+ \left( \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial F_3} {\partial x}\right) \vec \jmath + \left( \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial y}\right) \vec k. \end{align*}$

It has the following properties.
  • The direction of $latex \text{curl}\vec F$ at a point $latex P$ is the direction around which the circulation density of $latex \vec F$ is the greatest.
  • The magnitude of $latex \text{curl} \vec F$ is the circulation density around that direction.
  • The circulation density of $latex \vec F$ around any unit vector $latex \vec n$ is equal to $latex \text{curl}\vec F\cdot\vec n$.
  • For any function $latex f$ with continuous second partial derivatives,

$latex \text{curl grad} f = \vec \nabla \times \vec \nabla f = \vec 0.$

  • Conversely, any smooth vector field defined on a domain with no codimension-one holes and whose curl is zero everywhere is a gradient field (this is the curl test).

Stokes's and Green's theorems

The flux of a vector field $latex \vec F$ through an oriented surface $latex S$ is $latex \iint_S \vec F \cdot d\vec S$.
  • If the surface $latex S$ is the graph of a function $latex f(x,y)$, then, if $latex R$ is the domain of $latex f(x,y)$, we may write

$latex \displaystyle \iint_S \vec F \cdot d\vec S = \iint_R \vec F\left(x,y,z(x,y)\right)\cdot \left[-\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \vec \imath - \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \vec \jmath + \vec k \right] dx dy,$

  • If $latex S$ is a parametric surface parametrized by $latex \vec r = \vec r(s,t)$ where $latex s$ and $latex t$ vary in a region $latex R$, and if we assume that $latex \displaystyle \frac{\partial \vec r}{\partial s} \times \frac{\partial \vec r} {\partial t}$ points in the direction of the normal $latex \vec n$ to the surface $latex S$ everywhere, then we may write

$latex \displaystyle \int_S \vec F \cdot d\vec S = \int_R \vec F\left(\vec r(s,t)\right) \cdot \left(\frac{\partial \vec r}{\partial s} \times \frac{\partial \vec r} {\partial t} \right) ds dt.$

Stokes's theorem

Stokes's theorem links the circulation of a smooth vector field around a (piecewise) smooth closed curve $latex \mathcal C$ to the flux of $latex \text{curl} \vec F$ through any smooth surface $latex S$ whose boundary is equal to $latex \mathcal C$. It reads:

$latex \displaystyle \int_{\mathcal C} \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \int_S \text{curl}\vec F \cdot d\vec A,$

where the orientation of $latex \mathcal C$ is determined from the orientation of $latex S$ (or vice-versa) by the right hand-rule. Note that Stokes's theorem is valid only if $latex \vec F$ is defined everywhere on $latex S$ and $latex \mathcal C$.

Green's theorem

Green's theorem is a planar version of Stokes's theorem and reads

$latex \displaystyle \int_{\mathcal C} \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \int_R \left(\frac{\partial F_2}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial y} \right) \ dx\ dy,$

where $latex \vec F(x,y) = F_1(x,y) \vec \imath + F_2(x,y) \vec \jmath$ is a smooth vector field defined at every point of $latex \mathcal C$ as well as inside $latex \mathcal C$, and $latex \mathcal C$ is a (simple) closed curve oriented such that its interior $latex R$ is on the left as one moves along $latex \mathcal C$.

The divergence theorem

The divergence of a vector field $latex \vec F = F_1 \vec \imath+ F_2 \vec \jmath + F_3 \vec k$ with continuous partial derivatives is given by

$latex \text{div} \vec F = \vec \nabla \cdot \vec F = \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial F_2} {\partial y} + \frac{\partial F_3}{\partial z}.$

Note that it is a scalar quantity. It has the following properties.
  • $latex \displaystyle \text{div}(\vec \nabla f)= {\vec \nabla}^2 f = \Delta f = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial z^2}$.
  • $latex \displaystyle \text{div}(f \vec \nabla g) = f {\vec \nabla}^2 g + \vec \nabla f \cdot \vec \nabla g.$
  • $latex \displaystyle \hbox{div}(f \vec g)= f \hbox{div}(\vec g) + \vec \nabla f \cdot \vec g$.
  • The divergence of $latex \vec F$ at a point $latex P$ is equal to the limit, when the surface $latex S$ surrounding $latex P$ is shrunk to zero, of the flux of $latex \vec F$ through $latex S$ (oriented outward) divided by the volume of the region $latex W$ bounded by $latex S$. In other words,

$latex \text{div} \vec F = \displaystyle \lim_{W \rightarrow 0} \frac{\int_S \vec F \cdot d \vec A} {\hbox{volume of }W}.$

  • For any vector field $latex \vec F$ with continuous second partial derivatives,

$latex \text{div curl} \vec F = \vec \nabla \cdot (\vec \nabla \times \vec F) = 0.$

  • Conversely, any smooth vector field $latex \vec F$ whose domain is closed and has no holes, and whose divergence is zero everywhere is a curl field, i.e. there exists a vector field $latex \vec G$ such that $latex \vec F = \text{curl} \vec G$ (this is the divergence test).
The divergence theorem relates the flux of a smooth vector field through a (piecewise) smooth closed surface $latex S$ oriented outward to the volume integral of its divergence over the region $latex W$ bounded by $latex S$, and reads

$latex \displaystyle \int_S \vec F \cdot d \vec A \ = \int_W \hbox{div}\vec F \ dV,$

assuming that $latex \vec F$ is defined at every point in $latex W$ and on $latex S$.

Example: the continuity equation

Consider the flow $latex \vec v(\vec r)$ of a fluid of density $latex \rho(\vec r,t)$. Call $latex V$ a fixed region of the fluid domain. The mass of fluid in $latex V$ is given by $latex \displaystyle m = \int_V \rho dV$, and its rate of change is

$latex \displaystyle \frac{d m}{d t} = \int_V \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t}\ dV,$

if one assumes that $latex \rho$ is smooth. On the other hand, this rate of change is given by the negative of the flux of matter $latex \rho \vec v$ through the boundary $latex S$ of $latex V$, and by virtue of the divergence theorem,

$latex \displaystyle \frac{d m}{d t} = - \int_S \rho\ \vec v \cdot d\vec A = - \int_V \text{div}(\rho\ \vec v)\ dV.$

By comparison of the two expressions of $latex dm/dt,$ which are equal for every region $latex V$, we get the continuity equation, which reads

$latex \displaystyle \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \hbox{div}(\rho\ \vec v) = 0.$
